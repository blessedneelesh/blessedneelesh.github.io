Term project: AutoPlex Motors

A dealership management system that covers how a car dealer sells vehicles, buys inventory, collects payments and keeps its books.

The problem

A dealership juggles a lot of moving parts: customers, salespeople, vendors, vehicles, invoices, payments and bank activity. When those live in separate spreadsheets, sales get recorded wrong, nobody is sure what a customer still owes, and inventory costs get lost. I set out to design one system where each piece of information is entered once and stays accurate.

Status report: This semester I took the project from a set of user stories through requirements, a full database design and the first phases of object design. The foundation is in place, and the next step is building features on top of it one small piece at a time.

What the system lets people do

Manage sales invoices

A salesperson creates, edits and voids invoices tied to one vehicle and one customer, so every sale is recorded and mistakes can be corrected.

Calculate totals automatically

Price, taxes, fees and discounts roll up into an invoice total and a balance due, with no manual math.

Track payments

An accountant records partial and full payments, and the balance updates, so the dealership always knows who still owes money.

Record vehicle purchases

A purchasing manager logs the VIN, stock number, vendor and cost, and the vehicle is added to inventory with its acquisition cost captured once.

What I built this semester

  1. User stories and requirements. I wrote small, sequenced user stories and converted them into testable "shall" requirements with IDs.
  2. Database design. I modeled the system as an ER diagram in PlantUML covering banking, accounting and ledger, people, sales, and purchasing and inventory.
  3. PostgreSQL scripts. I generated the table definitions with auto-incrementing keys, plus sample data with valid foreign keys for testing.
  4. Object design. I created CRC cards and class diagrams, starting with master data (customers, employees, vendors) and then the sales invoice phase.
  5. Incremental delivery plan. I broke the work into small merges to the main branch, so early features never depend on tables that don't exist yet.

What I learned

Planning in small, ordered pieces saves rework. Getting the data model right before writing features made every later step clearer, and writing requirements in a strict format exposed gaps I would have missed otherwise.
